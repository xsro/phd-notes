\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{F}{ree-floating} space manipulators are enabling technologies for on-orbit servicing, active debris removal, in-space assembly, and spacecraft maintenance~\cite{liu2021review,yoshida2003etsvii}. Before contact with a target, however, the manipulator base is not rigidly supported. Joint motion therefore produces a reaction motion of the base through conservation of momentum, and the end-effector motion is coupled to the motion of the entire spacecraft--manipulator system~\cite{dubowsky1993,papadopoulos1991nature}. This coupling, together with nonlinear dynamics, uncertain parameters, exogenous disturbances, and actuator limits, makes accurate end-effector tracking substantially more difficult than tracking with a fixed-base robot. The difficulty is increased when the tracking error must be reduced within a time specified by the designer rather than by the controller gains or the initial condition.

\subsection{Modeling and kinematic reduction}
The coupled kinematics of space manipulators are commonly described by the generalized Jacobian, which combines the arm Jacobian with the momentum-consistent base reaction~\cite{umetani1989}, while the virtual-manipulator formulation provides an equivalent representation of the kinematic and dynamic coupling~\cite{vafa1990}. Fundamental properties of free-floating systems, including dynamic singularities, were established in~\cite{papadopoulos1993}, and singularity-free formulations of the equations of motion were later developed on the configuration space of the spacecraft--manipulator system~\cite{from2011singularity}. Because the generalized Jacobian becomes singular along configuration-dependent loci, workspace analyses and singularity-avoidance strategies have been studied extensively~\cite{nanos2015avoiding,nanos2023fullpose}. When the initial momentum is nonzero, the momentum constraint defines an augmented model, and the reachable workspace shrinks accordingly~\cite{nanos2017momentum,nanos2023fullpose}. Adaptive and dynamically equivalent manipulator models provide linear-parameterised surrogates that are convenient for identification and control~\cite{parlaktuna2004,yan}. Under the zero-momentum condition considered in this paper, the base motion can be eliminated, yielding an equivalent joint-space model of the form
\begin{equation}
 H_g(q)\ddot q+C_g(q,\dot q)\dot q=\tau_m .
\label{eq:intro_reduced_dynamics}
\end{equation}
This reduction is useful for controller design, but it does not remove the effects of model uncertainty, of the base reaction, or of lumped disturbances; it only transfers them to the torque channel.

\subsection{From trajectory planning to trajectory tracking}
A large body of work addresses the planning layer: base-attitude disturbance minimisation and reactionless motion~\cite{yoshida2001zeroreaction,zong2020reactionless,xu2016,zhan2022detumbling}, particle-swarm and evolutionary optimisation of joint paths~\cite{wang2015}, hierarchical task-priority planning with joint and obstacle constraints~\cite{cai2022hierarchical}, and model predictive planning with obstacle avoidance~\cite{wang2016nmpc,yang2025distributionally}. These methods generate feasible or even dynamically optimal references, but they do not, by themselves, guarantee that the executed trajectory coincides with the planned one in the presence of uncertainty.

For the tracking layer, adaptive control is a natural first choice. Experimental validation of adaptive robust controllers on a free-floating manipulator was reported in~\cite{pazelli2011experimental}; adaptive Jacobian schemes avoid the need for base acceleration and end-effector velocity measurements~\cite{wang2012adaptivejacobian}, and adaptive coordinated designs combine parameter adaptation with prescribed performance envelopes~\cite{lu2019adaptivecoordinated}. Disturbance-observer-based robust control~\cite{chu2008dob}, time-delay estimation~\cite{yu2022}, and sliding-mode designs with finite-time disturbance observers~\cite{shao2021ntsm,dou2023fractional,dou2025fractional} provide stronger disturbance rejection, at the price of nonsmooth or high-gain injection terms. Fractional-order and resolved-acceleration variants improve the transient response of the resolved-motion framework~\cite{shao2021fractional}, and logarithmic sliding surfaces combined with time-delay estimation reduce chattering~\cite{wang2025tdelog}. Model predictive control and learning-based schemes can accommodate constraints and unknown objectives~\cite{ning2019,wang2016nmpc,lei2022drl}, but their performance depends on model accuracy, on the available computation, and on the richness of the training data~\cite{yan}.

\subsection{Designable convergence time}
Classical asymptotic designs leave the settling time to be tuned through the gains. Finite-time control removes this dependence in part by using fractional powers or terminal sliding surfaces~\cite{su2020,jia2020finite,shao2021ntsm}, but the settling-time bound still depends on the initial condition. Fixed-time control removes that dependence~\cite{polyakov2012,zuo2019}, and has been applied to free-floating manipulators with prescribed performance, actuator faults, input quantisation, and output constraints~\cite{hu2024constrainedfixed,li2025,hu2025preassigned}. Prescribed-time (or predefined-time) designs go one step further: the settling time is an explicit design parameter that is selected independently of the initial state. Representative results include predefined-time tracking on $SE(3)$ for a free-flying robot~\cite{xu2024se3predefined}, practical prescribed-time tracking for tendon-driven manipulators~\cite{zou2025practical}, and tunable predefined-time tracking with an extended state observer for cable-driven manipulators~\cite{zou2026tunable,xuPredefinedTimeTrackingControl2024}. A recurring obstacle is that the prescribed-time mechanisms rely on terminal nonsmooth terms or on gains that grow without bound near the terminal instant, which may produce discontinuous inputs, gain singularities, or numerical difficulties at the very instant at which the guarantee is claimed~\cite{yan,hu2024constrainedfixed}. In addition, most observer-based designs assume a known bound on the disturbance or on its derivative and offer no handle on the observation time~\cite{yan}.

\subsection{Constraints and the case for delayed feedback}
Actuator saturation, output constraints, and performance envelopes further restrict what a prescribed-time law can promise. Auxiliary-system and barrier-function approaches handle saturation and prescribed performance~\cite{yao2021,zhou2025saturated,tian2024multiple}, and fragility-avoidance formulations relax the sensitivity of prescribed-performance control to the initial error~\cite{zhou2025saturated}. Event-triggered and communication-aware implementations reduce the on-board load~\cite{zhuang2025event}, and fault-tolerant designs address actuator faults and free-swinging joint failures~\cite{li2025,fu2022faulttolerant}. The interaction between these mechanisms and a hard convergence-time specification, however, remains only partially understood; conservative feasibility conditions are typical when saturation, faults, and constraints coexist~\cite{hu2024constrainedfixed,zhou2025saturated}.

Delayed feedback offers an alternative mechanism that is smooth by construction. Since the introduction of delayed self-feedback~\cite{pyragas1992}, delay-based methods have been used to stabilise systems without discontinuous terminal nonlinearities. In particular, smooth periodic delayed feedback (PDF) can achieve fixed-time stabilisation of controllable linear time-delay systems by annihilating a finite-dimensional one-period state-transition operator~\cite{zhouFixedtimeStabilizationLinear2022}. The resulting control law is continuous, contains no fractional powers of the error, and its convergence mechanism is algebraic rather than asymptotic. Extending this mechanism to a nonlinear free-floating manipulator is nontrivial for three reasons: the nonlinear terms and the disturbances must first be mapped into a sufficiently accurate auxiliary input channel; the effect of imperfect compensation on the one-period map must be quantified; and the observation of the lumped disturbance must itself terminate at a designable instant. A prescribed-time disturbance observer is a natural interface for the last requirement~\cite{jiangPrescribedtimeDisturbanceObserver2024}.

\subsection{Scope and contributions}
The above observations motivate the following question: can a free-floating space manipulator be transformed into a controllable error channel for smooth PDF control while retaining an explicit prescribed-time guarantee in the ideal case and a quantitative bound in the practical case? This paper answers this question for the reduced joint-space model in~\eqref{eq:intro_reduced_dynamics}, and it evaluates the answer on a complete three-dimensional floating-base model whose parameters are taken from an existing seven-degree-of-freedom space manipulator.

The main contributions are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A momentum-based reduction is formulated to explicitly relate the base reaction motion to an equivalent joint-space model, thereby providing a clear control-oriented representation of the free-floating manipulator, including the momentum-consistent bias forces that are usually omitted when a nominal joint-space model is postulated.
    \item A prescribed-time disturbance observer and a two-stage timing scheme are developed. The observation time and the subsequent tracking time are specified independently, which makes the overall timing requirement explicit.
    \item A dynamic compensation and normalisation procedure converts the nonlinear tracking problem into a controllable auxiliary error channel suitable for smooth periodic delayed feedback.
    \item A Gramian-based gain construction is provided, together with a stability analysis and explicit practical error bounds that account for observation error, model mismatch, actuator limitations, and numerical discretisation.
    \item The design is evaluated on the spatial rigid-body model of a seven-degree-of-freedom space manipulator using the kinematic and dynamic parameters of~\cite{dou2025fractional}, with a full composite-rigid-body/recursive-Newton--Euler implementation and the tabulated $\pm20~\mathrm{N\,m}$ actuator limits. The study therefore provides a reproducible benchmark and reports the measured settling time, the peak torque, the saturation duty cycle, and the base reaction motion, which are the quantities that the literature most often omits~\cite{yan}.
\end{itemize}
